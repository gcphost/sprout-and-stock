# Sprocket & Stock — what the game is

## The promise

**Build a robot-run food factory and shop, then automate it until the flow of
goods becomes the machine.**

Sprocket & Stock is not principally a game about growing plants. Grow racks,
vats, and pens provide inputs, but the player fantasy is scaling an operation:
from carrying the first crate, to directing a dozen robotic workers, to laying
out a production line that handles the repeat work without them.

## The player arc

1. **Do the work.** Receive, carry, process, stock, and sell small amounts of
   goods yourself alongside one robot.
2. **Build a crew.** Hire robots with useful specialties and set their job
   priorities. They can collect inputs, cook, stock shelves, run tills, and
   move crates, but their walks and imperfect priorities become part of the
   problem.
3. **Outgrow the crew.** More robots help, but long repeated routes cost wages,
   floor space, and time. A busy shop exposes bottlenecks in the production
   chain and in delivery to the shelf.
4. **Automate the route.** Conveyors carry crates; loaders transfer them between
   a line and a fixture; sorters route the flow. Processing machines create
   multi-step chains, so layout and throughput matter.
5. **Run the whole system.** Make products from raw inputs, stock a shop people
   want to visit, and respond to changing demand without letting the factory
   jam.

## What players make

The game has real production chains, rather than raw goods becoming a finished
product in one action. Inputs can be processed into intermediate goods and then
into retail food: grain into flour and dough, milk into butter and cheese, and
animal inputs into cuts, mince, sausages, and bacon. The Former supplies another
route for manufactured meat products. The point is not to collect ingredients;
it is to decide which chain to build and how its crates travel between stages.

## What to emphasize publicly

- Robotic workers, priorities, and the move from one bot to a working crew.
- The moment a hand-carried route becomes a conveyor, loader, and sorter line.
- Inputs becoming recognisable products, then products reaching customers.
- Factory-layout decisions and bottlenecks in a live shop.

## What not to lead with

- "Farm" or "plant-growing" as the genre label.
- Exterior growing footage without machines, robots, or logistics context.
- A generic cosy-shop pitch that hides the production and automation systems.

Growing systems should be presented as one source of supply in a larger factory,
not as the game's destination.
